Micro and Macro Models of the Labour Market · Lecture 7 · The McCall model

Why say no to a job?

An unemployed worker receives an offer that pays more than her benefits. Turning it down can still be the smart move. Today we find out when, by how much, and what that means for unemployment insurance and for the wage dispersion we see in the data.

    Notation used today

    w
    a wage offer. One offer arrives each period, drawn independently from the same distribution.
    F(w), f(w)
    F(w) is the share of offers below w; f(w) is the density. 1 − F(w) is the share of offers above w.
    b
    income per period while unemployed (benefits or the value of free time).
    β
    discount factor: one unit of income next period is worth β today (0 < β < 1).
    W(w)
    value of accepting offer w: w every period forever, so W(w) = w/(1 − β).
    U
    value of being unemployed and searching, measured before the next offer arrives.
    w*

    𝔼[max(w − x, 0)]
    option value at threshold x: the expected amount by which the next offer beats x.

      2 The policy view

      What is a rule worth?

      In the game you used a rule, perhaps without noticing: accept any offer of at least some number c, reject the rest. Each c is a strategy. Three values, all in the same lifetime units as the game score, tell us which c is best.

      Takeaway

      The best threshold is where accepting c and rejecting c are worth exactly the same. Indifference at the margin is the first-order condition.

      Notes

      F(c) is the share of offers below c and f(c) its density. For a threshold c, the value before seeing an offer is \( Q(c) = \dfrac{\int_c^\infty W(w)\,dF(w) + F(c)\,b}{1-\beta F(c)} \). Differentiate: \( Q'(c) = \dfrac{f(c)\,\big[\,b+\beta Q(c) - W(c)\,\big]}{1-\beta F(c)} \).

      Raising c only changes what happens to offers exactly equal to c: they are now rejected. Rejecting is worth b + βQ(c), accepting is worth W(c). So the best threshold solves W(c) = b + βQ(c). At the optimum, b + βQ(c*) is the value of unemployment U, and the condition becomes the familiar W(w*) = U.

      The curve is flat at the top: a threshold of 65 or 85 still earns 97–98% of the best value. Being roughly right is cheap; being very wrong (accepting anything above b) costs about a quarter of lifetime value.

      3 Recursion

      One equation for an infinite future

      U is the value of being unemployed and searching, measured before tomorrow's offer. W(w) is the value of accepting an offer w. The value of searching equals today's benefit plus the discounted value of the better of the two tomorrow. Tomorrow's problem looks exactly like today's, so one number, U, summarises the whole future. The offer at which accepting and searching are worth the same is the reservation wage, w*.

      \[ \begin{gathered} \class{hv-U}{U} = \class{hv-b}{b} + \class{hv-beta}{\beta}\,\class{hv-E}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\class{hv-max}{\max}\big\{\class{hv-W}{W(w)},\;\class{hv-U}{U}\big\}\Big] \\[4pt] \class{hv-W}{W(w)} = \frac{w}{1-\beta} \end{gathered} \]

      Takeaway

      max{W(w), U} is a hockey stick. Its kink sits at the reservation wage w*, where W(w*) = U, and its convexity is where the option value comes from.

      Notes

      W(w) = w + βW(w) gives W(w) = w/(1−β), the present value of earning w forever. U is the value of being unemployed today, before tomorrow's draw. Because W rises with w and U does not depend on w, there is a single crossing: accept if and only if w ≥ w*, where W(w*) = U, that is w* = (1−β)U.

      The Bellman operator \( T(U) = b + \beta\,\mathbb{E}[\max\{W(w),U\}] \) is monotone and discounts (Blackwell), so it is a contraction with exactly one fixed point. Notice what the expectation averages: the maximum, not W. That is why the spread of offers will matter.

      4 Derivation

      Two tricks, one formula

      From the Bellman equation to the reservation-wage equation in seven moves, then one integration by parts that turns the option value into an area. The term that changes at each step is highlighted.

      F(w*)
      the probability that an offer is below w*, so it is rejected.
      dF(w)
      shorthand for f(w)dw, the probability of an offer close to w. ∫ g(w) dF(w) is the average of g over offers.
      w*
      the reservation wage, defined by W(w*) = U.
      \[ U = \class{t-b}{b} + \beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\max\{\class{t-W}{W(w)},\,\class{t-U}{U}\}\big] \]

      Start. Collect b today; tomorrow take the better of accepting and searching.

      \[ U = b + \beta\Big[\class{chg}{F(w^*)\,U} + \class{chg}{\int_{w^*}^{\infty} W(w)\,dF(w)}\Big] \]

      Use the threshold rule. Offers below w* are rejected and leave you with U; offers above are accepted and pay W(w).

      \[ \class{chg}{\frac{w^*}{1-\beta}} = b + \beta\Big[F(w^*)\,\class{chg}{\frac{w^*}{1-\beta}} + \int_{w^*}^{\infty}\class{chg}{\frac{w}{1-\beta}}\,dF(w)\Big] \]

      Substitute. W(w) = w/(1−β), and indifference at the threshold gives U = w*/(1−β).

      \[ w^* = \class{chg}{(1-\beta)}\,b + \beta\Big[F(w^*)\,w^* + \int_{w^*}^{\infty} w\,dF(w)\Big] \]

      Multiply by (1−β). Everything is now in wage units.

      \[ \int_{w^*}^{\infty} w\,dF(w) = \class{chg}{\int_{w^*}^{\infty}(w-w^*)\,dF(w)} + \class{chg}{w^*\,\big[1-F(w^*)\big]} \]

      Trick 1: add and subtract w*. Split every accepted wage into the threshold plus the excess over it.

      \[ w^* = (1-\beta)\,b + \class{chg}{\beta\,w^*} + \beta\int_{w^*}^{\infty}(w-w^*)\,dF(w) \]

      Collect terms. βF(w*)w* + βw*[1−F(w*)] = βw*: the threshold earns βw* whatever happens.

      \[ \boxed{\;w^* = \class{t-b}{b} + \frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\,\class{t-opt}{\int_{w^*}^{\infty}(w-w^*)\,dF(w)}\;} \]

      Solve for w*. Flow value of unemployment plus the capitalised option value of searching once more.

      \[ \int_{w^*}^{\infty}(w-w^*)\,dF(w) = \class{chg}{\Big[-(w-w^*)\big(1-F(w)\big)\Big]_{w^*}^{\infty}} + \int_{w^*}^{\infty}\big[1-F(w)\big]\,dw \]

      Trick 2: integrate by parts with dF = −d(1−F). The boundary term is zero at both ends.

      \[ w^* = \class{t-b}{b} + \frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\,\class{t-opt}{\int_{w^*}^{\infty}\big[1-F(w)\big]\,dw} \]

      Read it as geometry. The option value is the area under the survival curve 1−F to the right of w*. Next scene: see it.

      Notes

      Why does the integral start at w*? Offers below the threshold are rejected anyway, so they add nothing beyond U. Why w − w* and not w? The gain from a good draw is how much it beats the marginal acceptable offer. Why β/(1−β)? β because the draw happens tomorrow; 1/(1−β) because a better wage, once accepted, is paid forever.

      Hand check with offers uniform on [0, 100], b = 20, β = 0.95: the integral is (100 − w*)²/200, so w* = 20 + 19·(100 − w*)²/200, a quadratic with root w* = 75.77.

At the default values, 20 more units of benefit raise w* by about 3.8.

      6 Computation

      How does a computer find w*?

      For most offer distributions the reservation-wage equation has no closed form. A program has to do two jobs. First, describe F with finitely many numbers. Second, solve the equation, either by repeating the Bellman update until it settles, or by searching for the root. You will rarely write this code yourself. You need to know what it does, so that you can tell when it is wrong.

      Notes
      
    
      Discretizing. The notebook represents the lognormal offer distribution by 200 equally likely points. Quantile midpoints avoid the extreme tails, where a few points would carry too much weight. The grid error shrinks quickly: with uniform offers, 20 points already get w* right to about 0.1.
      
    
      Iterating. The notebook iterates \( U_{n+1} = b + \beta\,\mathbb{E}[\max\{W(w), U_n\}] \). Multiply by (1−β) and write x = (1−β)U: you get \( x_{n+1} = m(x_n) \). Since \( m'(x) = \beta F(x) \in [0, \beta) \), the error falls at least by the factor β per step and, close to the solution, by βF(w*). The starting value does not matter, but patient workers are slow to solve: as β → 1 the rate approaches 1.
      
    
      Bisecting. Bisection needs only two facts: h is continuous and changes sign once (scene 5 showed g is decreasing, so h is increasing). Its speed, a factor ½ per step, does not depend on β. Here it beats iteration (factor 0.72), and for β = 0.99 it wins by far. Iteration has one advantage: it generalises to problems where the unknown is a whole function, not one number, as in Lectures 9 and 11.

      9 Intuition
      
    
      Why risk can be good news
      
    
      Rejecting an offer buys a call option on next period's draw. Bad draws are thrown away; good draws are kept. Like any option, it is worth more when the underlying is more volatile. Risk aversion pushes the other way. To measure risk aversion we use utility u(w) = w1−γ/(1−γ): γ = 0 is the risk-neutral worker of the previous scenes, and a higher γ means more risk aversion.

      Notes
      
    
      max(w − x, 0) is convex in w. By Jensen's inequality, any mean-preserving spread raises 𝔼[max(w − x, 0)] at every x, so it raises g(x) and w*. With lognormal offers, \( d_1 = [\ln(M/x) + \sigma^2/2]/\sigma \) and \( d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \): the option value of search is the Black–Scholes price of a call with strike x, zero interest and unit maturity.
      
    
      Risk aversion lowers w*, and here is a three-line proof. With concave u, the reservation wage solves \( u(w^*) - u(b) = \frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\mathbb{E}[\max\{u(w) - u(w^*), 0\}] \). At the risk-neutral threshold, concavity gives \( u(w^*) - u(b) \ge u'(w^*)(w^* - b) \) and \( u(w) - u(w^*) \le u'(w^*)(w - w^*) \), so the left side exceeds the right side. The left minus the right rises with the threshold, so the risk-averse root lies below. In this basic model (no savings) the result holds for every concave u; the hedge on the slides is needed only once savings or other margins enter.
      
    
      The right panel combines both forces: with γ = 8 the spread lowers w* (from 30.5 at s = 5 to 29.1 at s = 50), because concavity now outweighs the option.

      How hard to search, and how picky to be

The job-finding rate (or hazard) is the probability of leaving unemployment this period: an offer must arrive, p(e*), and pass the threshold, 1 − F(w*).       Notes
      
    
      \( U = \max_e \{ b - c(e) + \beta[\,U + p(e)\,G\,] \} \), with \( G = \mathbb{E}[\max\{W(w) - U, 0\}] \) the expected gain from one contact. First-order condition: \( c'(e^*) = \beta\,p'(e^*)\,G \): marginal cost equals the expected gain from one more contact. Here that reads κe = βe−eG.

The hazard \( h = p(e^*)[1 - F(w^*)] \) falls through both factors, and the bar chart splits the fall: \( \Delta\ln h = \Delta\ln p(e^*) + \Delta\ln[1-F(w^*)] \). Insurance value against behavioural distortion is a quantitative question; the model tells us which channels to measure.
      
  
      

      



      
  
      
    
      11 Policy and evidence
      
    
      What happens as benefits run out?
      
    
      Benefits b last T periods, then fall to b₀. The worker knows the date. The reservation wage now depends on the period of the spell, w*ₜ, and so does the hazard: the probability of finding a job in period t, given that you are still unemployed, 1 − F(w*ₜ). We solve the model and compare two predictions with the evidence.

      How the code solves it
      
        
      Backward induction, no iteration needed.
      
        
      1. After exhaustion the problem is stationary: solve for w* with b₀ forever, as in scene 6.
      
        
      2. Step back one period at a time: w*ₜ = (1−β)bₜ + β[w*ₜ₊₁ + 𝔼 max(w − w*ₜ₊₁, 0)]. Each period uses the answer for the next one.
      
        
      3. Hazard in period t: 1 − F(w*ₜ). Expected duration: the sum of the probabilities of still being unemployed at the start of each period.
      
      
      
      
      
        
      What the data say
      
        
        
          
      • Durations: the model passes. Longer potential benefit duration raises nonemployment duration in four regression-discontinuity designs. Survey figure in Le Barbanchon, Schmieder & Weber (2024).
        • 
          
      • The spike: partly an accounting effect. Exits from the UI register spike at exhaustion much more than re-employment does. Card, Chetty & Weber (2007, AER P&P).
        • 
          
      • The fall-back: not in this model. Hazards rise at benefit cuts and then decline, which reference-dependent preferences fit better. DellaVigna, Lindner, Reizer & Schmieder (2017, QJE).
        • 
          
      • The mechanism: weak direct support. Reported reservation wages do not respond to benefit duration, and fall only 0.05–0.14% per week over the spell. Le Barbanchon, Rathelot & Roulet (2019, JPubE); Krueger & Mueller (2016, AEJ: Policy).
        • 
        
      
      
      
    
      
  
      
  
      
    
      Notes
      
    
      Backward induction in wage units: \( w^*_t = (1-\beta)\,b_t + \beta\,\big[w^*_{t+1} + \mathbb{E}\max(w - w^*_{t+1}, 0)\big] \), starting from the stationary post-exhaustion value. The reservation wage falls as exhaustion approaches, and the largest drop happens in the last period, when the next period's benefit falls from b to b₀.
      
    
      Prediction 1: the hazard rises towards T and then stays at the higher post-exhaustion level. The basic model does not produce a spike that falls back. Prediction 2: a longer T raises expected duration. The data support prediction 2; the shape of the hazard and the flat reservation wages point to search effort and reference dependence as important channels. This is a good place to say that McCall does not tell us whether UI is good or bad; it tells us which behavioural responses to measure.
      
  
      

      



      
  
      
    
      12 A quantitative test
      
    
      How much wage dispersion can search explain?
      
    
      Hornstein, Krusell and Violante (2011) noticed that the reservation-wage equation pins down the ratio of the average accepted wage to the lowest one. It needs only worker flows and preferences, no wage data at all.
      
    
      
      
      w̄ and w*
      the average accepted wage and the lowest accepted wage (the reservation wage). Their ratio is the mean-min ratio.
      
      
      h
      the monthly probability that an unemployed worker finds a job, 1 − F(w*).
      
      
      s
      the monthly probability of losing a job. With s = 0 we are back in this lecture's model.
      
      
      ρ
      the value of unemployment as a share of the average wage, b / w̄.
      
      
      k
      a combination of flows and patience, defined in the formula below.
      
    
      
  
      
  
      
    
      
      
      \[ \frac{\bar w}{w^*} \;=\; \frac{1+k}{\rho + k}, \qquad k = \frac{\beta\,h}{1-\beta(1-s)}, \qquad \rho = \frac{b}{\bar w} \]

      Takeaway
      
        
      Spells are short, so waiting cannot be worth much, so offers cannot be very dispersed. Search alone explains about a 5% gap; the data show 50–100%.
      
      
      
    
      
  
      
  
      
    
      Notes
      
    
      Write b = ρw̄ with w̄ = 𝔼[w | w ≥ w*], and h = 1 − F(w*). The reservation-wage equation becomes w* = ρw̄ + k(w̄ − w*), so w̄/w* = (1 + k)/(ρ + k). The offer distribution drops out: everything we need about F is in the job-finding rate. Allowing jobs to end with probability s replaces β/(1−β) by β/(1−β(1−s)) in the reservation-wage equation; with s = 0 you get this lecture's model.
      
    
      US monthly flows (h = 0.43, s = 0.03, r = 5%) give k ≈ 12.6 and a mean-min ratio of 1.046, exactly the value in HKV. Residual wage dispersion implies 1.5–2. To reach 1.5 with these flows, ρ would have to be about −3.5: unemployment would have to feel like paying three and a half average wages every month. On-the-job search breaks the link, because accepting no longer ends the search (Lecture 9: about 1.25). Counteroffers can push the ratio above 2 (Lecture 11). The scoreboard will come back.
      
  
      

      



      
  
      
    
      13 Wrap-up
      
    
      What to remember
      
  
      
  
      
    
      
      
        
        
      1. Unemployment can be an investment. The worker rejects offers above b because accepting kills the option to draw again.
        2. 
        
      2. One number summarises the strategy. w* = b + β/(1−β) × option value, where the option value is the area under 1 − F to the right of w*.
        3. 
        
      3. Everything works through the option value. Benefits, patience and dispersion raise it; risk aversion and effort costs change how it is valued; finite UI makes it fall over the spell.
        4. 
        
      4. The model is testable, and it fails in instructive ways. Durations respond to benefits as predicted; reservation wages barely move; and search alone generates almost no wage dispersion.
        5. 
      
      
      
      The reservation wage makes the worker indifferent between a job now and the option to keep searching; everything in the model follows from what changes that option value.

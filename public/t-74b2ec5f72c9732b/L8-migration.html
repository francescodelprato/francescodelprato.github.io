Micro and Macro Models of the Labour Market · Lecture 8 · Matching, scarce jobs and migration

Can migration raise unemployment?

When new workers arrive in a local labor market, do local workers find jobs less often? The textbook matching model says no. The evidence says yes, often by a lot, and more in bad times. Today we rebuild the matching model so that it can answer the question, and then we ask what migration policy should do.

    1 The question

    100 newcomers arrive. How many locals lose out?

    Suppose 100 new workers arrive in a local labor market for reasons that have nothing to do with local conditions: a war, a border that opens, colonists who return home. They are just like the local workers. How many local workers end up unemployed because of them?

    Eleven migration experiments

    Unemployed local workers per 100 arrivals. Bars show the range of estimates in each study, dots the midpoint. Compiled by Michaillat (2024, table 1).

    range of estimatesmidpointaverage: 37our guess

    Before you look

    Agree on a number as a class.

    Two caricatures

    Jobs scale up. Firms create a job for every newcomer who would be employed. No local loses out: 0.

    Jobs are fixed. Every employed newcomer holds a job that a local would otherwise have held: about 96.

    Takeaway

    On average, 100 arrivals push 37 locals into unemployment, with a range from 15 to 72. The textbook matching model predicts zero. Today we find out why, and what changes the answer.

    Notes

    Some studies measure the effect per 100 arrivals in the labor force, others per 100 arrivals who found a job; ranges come from different specifications, and the summary uses midpoints (mean 37, median 31). All are short-run effects in local labor markets. In the long run, firms adjust capital and the effect fades; immigration also raises innovation. The "jobs are fixed" caricature gives 100 × (1 − u) with u = 4.3%: every employed newcomer replaces a local.

    Sources: Card (1990); Hunt (1992); Boustan, Fishback & Kantor (2010); Makela (2017); Borjas & Monras (2017, two estimates); Glitz (2012); D'Amuri, Ottaviano & Peri (2010); Angrist & Kugler (2003); Dustmann, Schönberg & Stuhler (2017); Labanca (2016). Public opinion points the same way: in the 1992 American National Election Studies, more than 80% of respondents thought it at least somewhat likely that immigrants take jobs away from people already here.

    2 Recap: matching

    Given tightness, how many people work?

    Jobseekers and vacancies meet through a matching function. One number, tightness, sums up the market. Jobs end at a constant rate. When the flow into employment equals the flow out, tightness alone pins down the unemployment rate.

    Takeaway

    On the worker side, tightness is the whole story: flows pin down u(θ) = δ/(δ + f(θ)), whatever the size of the labor force. Employment is then Lˢ = (1 − u(θ))H, the labor supply of the matching model.

    Notes

    Employment moves as \( \dot L = f(\theta)\,U - \delta L \): jobseekers find jobs at rate f, workers lose jobs at rate δ. Flows balance when \( f(\theta)(H - L) = \delta L \), which gives \( L^s(\theta, H) = \frac{f(\theta)}{\delta + f(\theta)}\,H \) and \( u(\theta) = \frac{\delta}{\delta + f(\theta)} \). Michaillat calls this locus the labor supply: it is the employment level consistent with balanced flows, not a labor-leisure choice. In the unemployment-vacancy plane the same locus is the Beveridge curve.

    Rates and probabilities: with a rate f per quarter, the chance of finding a job within a quarter is 1 − e−f, and the average search lasts 1/f quarters. Flows are fast (δ + f ≈ 2.3 per quarter), so deviations from balance die out within weeks; scene 10 simulates this. The elasticity of labor supply with respect to tightness is \( \epsilon^s_\theta = (1-\eta)\,u(\theta) \): small, because u is small.

    3 The cost of hiring

    Hiring takes workers

    A vacancy does not fill itself: someone has to screen, interview and train. Suppose each vacancy takes κ = 1 recruiter, a full-time worker. A firm that wants to keep its size must replace the workers who leave each quarter. In a tight market vacancies fill slowly, so the firm needs more of them, and more recruiters.

    Takeaway

    Hiring costs workers' time, and the cost rises with tightness. Each producer comes with τ(θ) recruiters, so her full cost is (1 + τ(θ))w, not just the wage w.

    Notes

    This is the vacancy cost of Part 1, measured in workers' time instead of output: k/q(θ) there, κδ/q(θ) of the workforce here. The calibration κ = 1 (one full-time worker per vacancy) follows Michaillat and Saez. The elasticity of 1 + τ with respect to tightness is \( \epsilon^{1+\tau}_\theta = \eta\,\tau(\theta) \): small in slack markets, larger in tight ones. That fact drives the rest of the lecture.

    τ(θ) explodes when q(θ) approaches κδ, at θ ≈ 642 here, far beyond anything observed.

    4 The textbook benchmark

    In the textbook model, newcomers change nothing

    In the textbook model every producer adds the same output, a. Firms open vacancies as long as a producer is worth more than her full cost: the wage w plus the wages of the τ(θ) recruiters she needs, (1 + τ(θ))w. Free entry pushes the two together: a = (1 + τ(θ))w. That equation fixes tightness whatever the number of workers, so labor demand is a horizontal line. Now let newcomers arrive.

    Stays put. Tightness is fixed by a = (1 + τ(θ))w, so the job-finding rate and the unemployment rate do not change. Raise σ: if wages fall, tightness and job finding rise, and unemployment falls.

    Takeaway

    With flat labor demand, tightness cannot move. Firms open vacancies in proportion to the newcomers and absorb them at the old unemployment rate. If wages fall, unemployment even falls.

    Notes

    The same neutrality holds in the Part 1 version with a vacancy cost k and Nash bargaining: the job-creation condition \( k/q(\theta) = (p - w)/(r + \delta) \) and the wage \( w = (1-\beta)b + \beta(p + k\theta) \) contain no H, so θ does not depend on the size of the labor force. It holds for any wage rule (Michaillat 2024, figure 5). In the employment-tightness plane, the textbook model looks like the competitive "Cardian" model in the employment-wage plane: a horizontal demand curve absorbs any supply shift.

    The model is internally coherent; it is simply too insensitive to answer the migration question. The 100 newcomers in the grid split into those who are unemployed (at the old rate) and those who fill brand-new jobs. Nobody takes a job a local would have had.

    5 Scarce jobs

    When each extra worker is worth less

    In the short run machines and offices are fixed, so output is y = aP1−α: each extra producer adds less than the one before. A firm hires producers until the marginal product equals the full cost, (1 − α)aP−α = (1 + τ(θ))w. A tighter market raises the full cost, so the firm wants fewer workers. Labor demand now slopes down.

    a, α = 0.35
    productivity, and decreasing returns to labor. The labor share is 1 − α = 65%.
    w = ω a1−γ H−σ
    a rigid wage norm. γ = 0.3: wages move less than one for one with productivity. σ ≥ 0: wages may fall when the labor force grows; σ = 0 in the baseline, because wages respond little to migration in the data.
    labor supplylabor demand with your αtextbook demand (α → 0)
    \[ \max_{P}\; aP^{1-\alpha} - \big[1+\tau(\theta)\big]\,w\,P \]

    Profits. Output minus the full cost of producers, recruiters included.

    \[ (1-\alpha)\,a\,P^{-\alpha} = \big[1+\tau(\theta)\big]\,w \]

    First-order condition. The marginal product of a producer equals her full cost.

    \[ L^d(\theta, a) = \left\{ \frac{(1-\alpha)\,a}{\big[1+\tau(\theta)\big]^{1-\alpha}\,w} \right\}^{1/\alpha} \]

    Labor demand. Use L = (1 + τ(θ))P to count employees instead of producers.

    \[ \epsilon^d_\theta = \frac{d\ln L^d}{d\ln\theta} = -\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\eta\,\tau(\theta) \;<\; 0 \]

    The slope. Demand falls with tightness. It is steep when τ is small (slack markets) and flatter when τ is large (tight markets).

    Takeaway

    Two ingredients make jobs scarce: diminishing returns, and a hiring cost that rises with tightness. With a rigid wage, more jobseekers no longer bring proportionally more jobs.

    Notes

    Why rigid wages? In the matching model wages are set within a bilateral monopoly, so any wage in the bargaining set is consistent with equilibrium, and a wage norm can be disciplined by evidence. Immigration has small effects on local wages (Ottaviano and Peri 2012; Card 2012), so the baseline sets σ = 0. Proposition 2 in Michaillat (2024) shows that all results survive for σ ∈ [0, α); they disappear only if wages are fully flexible, σ = α.

    Job rationing. Even if hiring were free (θ → 0, so τ → 0), firms would employ only Lᵈ(0) workers. When that number is below the labor force, part of unemployment is not frictional: it would persist without any matching frictions (Michaillat 2012). As α → 0 the demand curve flattens into the textbook horizontal line; at α = 0.35 it is steep where τ is small.

    6 The migration experiment

    Newcomers lower everyone's chance of finding a job

    Add arrivals equal to a share of the labor force. They shift labor supply to the right; labor demand does not move. Tightness falls until supply and demand meet again, so the job-finding rate falls for every jobseeker, locals included.

    Do local workers lose their jobs faster after the arrivals?

    No. The job-loss rate δ does not change. Locals who lose a job stay unemployed longer, because job finding is slower. Dustmann, Schönberg & Stuhler (2017) find exactly this for Czech commuters in Germany: fewer hires of German jobseekers, not more layoffs.

    Takeaway

    Newcomers push nobody out of a job. They make it harder for every jobseeker to find one. Wages that respond soften the blow by the factor 1 − σ/α and spread it to employed workers.

    Notes

    Differentiate Lˢ(θ, H) = Lᵈ(θ, a, H) with w = ωa1−γH−σ: \( \epsilon^\theta_H = -\left(1 - \frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{\epsilon^s_\theta - \epsilon^d_\theta} \). Because εs is small (about 0.02 at θ = 1) and εd is moderate (about −0.04), a 1% inflow cuts tightness by about 15%. The percentage-point rise in unemployment per 1% inflow is \( \frac{du}{d\ln H} = \left(1-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\frac{1-u}{1 - \epsilon^d_\theta/\epsilon^s_\theta} \); multiplied by 100 it gives the locals pushed into unemployment per 100 arrivals.

    Firms gain: lower tightness means fewer recruiters per producer and more producers, and profits are \( \pi = \alpha\,a\,P^{1-\alpha} \) (Proposition 3). The model joins the two camps of the immigration literature: like Borjas, labor demand slopes down; like Card, wages need not fall. The adjustment happens through tightness.

    7 Good times, bad times, and the evidence

    Why the damage is larger in a slump

    Bad times are periods of low labor demand: productivity falls while wages stay rigid, the demand curve shifts left, and tightness is low. Run the same small inflow at different points of the cycle and compare with the experiments.

    Where does a given inflow push more locals into unemployment?

    In a slump. At tightness 0.5 about 48 locals per 100 arrivals, at tightness 2 about 20.

    What the experiments suggest

    • Tight markets, small effects. France 1962 (unemployment about 1%): 20 per 100. Portugal 1974–77 (about 2%): 22.
    • Slack markets, large effects. Europe in the 1990s (unemployment about 10%): 59 per 100 at the midpoint of Angrist & Kugler (2003).
    • Regions. Effects are weaker in regions with good economic conditions (Edo & Ozguzel 2023).

    Takeaway

    In a slump vacancies fill fast, recruiting costs little, and it barely gets cheaper when jobseekers multiply. Firms hardly expand: jobs are close to fixed, and newcomers mostly add to unemployment.

    Notes

    Proposition 5 gives the elasticity of the employment rate l = 1 − u with respect to the labor force: \( \epsilon^l_H = -\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\right)\Big/\left(1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\,\frac{\tau(\theta)}{u(\theta)}\right) \). As θ → 0, τ/u → 0 and the elasticity tends to −1: the fixed-jobs caricature. At efficient tightness it equals −(α − σ). As θ grows, it tends to 0: the textbook model. The model therefore contains both caricatures from scene 1 as limits.

    The episode markers are placed where the model curve reaches the measured effect, which is Michaillat's own reading; a real test would measure tightness in each episode, which makes a good project question. Politics follows the same pattern: the shift of Austrian voters towards the anti-immigration FPÖ in response to immigration is about twice as large in local labor markets in the top quartile of unemployment as in the bottom quartile (Halla, Wagner & Zweimüller 2017). US tightness averaged 0.73 between 1930 and 2024; it reached 2 only during the post-pandemic boom.

    8 Efficiency

    How tight should a labor market be?

    Two kinds of labor produce nothing: jobseekers who are idle, and workers who are busy recruiting. A tighter market cuts the first and raises the second. The efficient tightness maximizes the share of the labor force that produces.

    Takeaway

    The market is efficient when ητ(θ) = (1 − η)u(θ). Under the US calibration that is about one vacancy per jobseeker, θ* ≈ 1. Below it, the market is inefficiently slack; above it, inefficiently tight.

    Notes

    Output depends on producers, \( P = \frac{1-u(\theta)}{1+\tau(\theta)}\,H \). Maximize the producing share: its elasticity with respect to θ is \( (1-\eta)u(\theta) - \eta\,\tau(\theta) \), so the optimum satisfies \( \eta\,\tau(\theta) = (1-\eta)\,u(\theta) \): the marginal gain from fewer idle workers equals the marginal cost of more recruiters. With κ = 1, recruiters equal vacancies, so at θ* there are about as many recruiters as jobseekers, u ≈ v. This is the Michaillat–Saez rule u* = √(uv).

    The same condition is the efficiency condition of the textbook model with zero interest and zero value of unemployment. With Nash bargaining, the textbook model reaches it when the worker's bargaining power equals η, the elasticity of matches with respect to jobseekers (the Hosios condition, β = η). A warning for testing code: with η = 0.5, the statements β = η and β = 1 − η give the same number, so a check at η = 0.5 cannot tell a correct formula from a wrong one. The US labor market has mostly been inefficiently slack: tightness averaged 0.73 between 1930 and 2024 (Michaillat and Saez 2024).

    9 Winners, losers and policy

    Who gains, and what should policy do?

    Firms gain from arrivals: recruiting gets easier and profits rise. Local workers lose: job finding falls, and wages fall too if σ > 0. Add the two: local income is local labor income plus local profits, with all firms owned locally.

    Takeaway

    Arrivals raise local income when the market is too tight and lower it when the market is too slack. The best migration policy is procyclical: admit more workers in booms, fewer in slumps.

    Notes

    With local labor force H and total labor force mH, local income is \( W = w\,l\,H + \pi \) with \( \pi = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,w\,l\,mH \). The elasticity of W with respect to m at m = 1 is \( (\alpha - \sigma)\left[1 - \frac{1}{\alpha + (1-\alpha)\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\frac{\tau}{u}}\right] \), which is zero exactly at the efficiency condition of scene 8. The welfare criterion leaves out distribution: workers who own no firms lose in every case.

    Starting from a tight market, the best inflow brings tightness to just below θ* (Proposition 7). In this implementation it is 1.35% of the labor force when tightness starts at 1.2 and 2.5% when it starts at 1.4; Michaillat reports about 1% and 2%. The policy could work like extended unemployment benefits in the US, which switch on automatically when state unemployment crosses a threshold; multi-year work visas fit the length of a typical business cycle.

    11 The same lesson in macro
    
    
    Why the textbook model barely moves over the cycle
    
    
    Shimer (2005) showed that the textbook model is too insensitive to productivity shocks as well. With Nash bargaining, wages absorb most of a productivity change, so the profit from a hire barely moves, and neither do vacancies. In this lecture's model, the wage rigidity γ governs exactly this margin.

    Notes
    
    
    With w = ωa1−γ, the wage-to-productivity ratio is ωa−γ, and \( \epsilon^\theta_a = \frac{\gamma}{\alpha\,(\epsilon^s_\theta - \epsilon^d_\theta)} \). At γ = 0, productivity cancels from labor demand and tightness does not move at all. At γ = 0.3, a 5% fall in productivity halves tightness (Michaillat 2024, section 9.2). The US estimate of 8.6 is from Michaillat (2012).
    
    
    In the textbook model with Nash bargaining (p: output per worker, b: value of non-work, β: the worker's bargaining power, r: the interest rate), \( \epsilon^\theta_p = \frac{p}{p-b}\cdot\frac{r+\delta+\beta f}{\eta(r+\delta)+\beta f} \). With Shimer's quarterly calibration (b/p = 0.4, β = η = 0.72, δ = 0.1, r = 0.012, f = 1.355) this is 1.7. Ljungqvist and Sargent (2017) show that every proposed fix (a high value of non-work as in Hagedorn–Manovskii, rigid wages as in Hall, alternating-offer bargaining as in Hall–Milgrom) works by shrinking the "fundamental surplus" p − b relative to p, or by stopping wages from absorbing the shock.

    What to remember
    
  
    
  
    
    
    
      
      
        
    1. The textbook matching model cannot see migration-induced unemployment. Labor demand is flat, so newcomers are absorbed at the old unemployment rate.
      2.

      3.
